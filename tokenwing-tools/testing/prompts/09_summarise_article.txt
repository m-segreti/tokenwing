The Amazon rainforest covers roughly 5.5 million square kilometres across nine countries, roughly nine
per cent of the world's land surface. About 390 billion individual trees grow there, and scientists
believe somewhere between three and four million species live inside the region -- around a quarter of
all the species on the planet. The trees pump about six billion tonnes of carbon dioxide out of the
atmosphere each year, which is close to ten per cent of the annual global total.

The river system is just as remarkable. The Amazon discharges roughly 209,000 cubic metres per second
into the Atlantic, more than the next seven largest rivers combined, and it carries an estimated
six to nine million tonnes of sediment to the ocean every year. That sediment load feeds a plume of
nutrients in the Atlantic which, in turn, supports an enormous fishery far from shore.

Deforestation remains the main threat. Satellite monitoring recorded roughly 1.5 million hectares of
primary forest loss in 2022 alone, the lowest figure in fifteen years but still an area roughly the
size of Delaware disappearing every year. Conservation efforts in Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador and Peru
have held total loss below the peaks of the early 2000s, but the long-term trend of roads and
fragmentation continues to raise the odds that the remaining forest is split into fragments too small
to support the species that depend on it.
